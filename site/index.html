gliim Unit 000 · Trying
Lighting for large indoor spaces

gliim

The dim-witted gnome that tries.

You turn a knob. A whole row of lights follows.
That is the whole of it.

62% turn it — drag around, or use arrow keys

The whole interface

One knob. One row.

Turn it and the whole row follows your hand together, all the way down to almost nothing — no stepping, no flicker at the bottom, which is where the useful settings live.

Press it and that row goes out. Press again and it comes back exactly where you left it. There is nothing to select first, nothing to remember, and nothing to install on a phone.

The knob does not have to be near the lights.

Across a room

A panel at every door.

A panel can sit by the near door, or on the far wall thirty paces away, joined by a cable thin enough to run behind a skirting board. Where a cable cannot go, a panel goes without one.

None of them needs to know about the others. Add one by the second entrance and it simply works — turn either knob and the same lights follow. Nobody has to be told which panel is in charge, because none of them is.

Rooms it is for

Places that outgrew the switch by the door.

Halls, dining rooms, workshops, studios, libraries, meeting rooms — spaces big enough that the light needs to change depending on what is happening in them, and where the person who wants it changed is rarely standing next to the one switch that does it.

Everyone already knows how to use it. That is not a small thing in a room full of people who did not read anything beforehand.

In fairness

What it will not do.

It will not sense the room. It will not learn your habits, notice the sunset, or form a view about your sleep. It has no clock and would not know what to do with one. It cannot be reached from a phone, and adding that would mean adding a great many things that go wrong when the internet does.

If the power fails it comes back on, at the level you left it. A light that boots into darkness is a light that looks broken, and it has enough shortcomings without inventing that one.

The unit

gliim-3

Three lights, three knobs, one small enclosure. Add panels until every door has one. The renders above are how it is meant to end up; the boards themselves are on the bench, which is a different and less photogenic stage of the process.

  • Rows three, each a run of lights dimmed as one
  • Control one knob per row — turn to dim, press for off
  • Panels as many as the room has doors
  • Link a thin two-pair cable, or none at all
  • Power one USB-C supply for the whole thing
